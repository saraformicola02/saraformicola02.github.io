Supervised Classification with a Support Vector Machine (RBF Kernel) of Landsat 8 Imagery in Google Earth Engine for Mapping Deforestation in the Bolivian Rainforest (2018-2025)

Machine learning is the branch of artificial intelligence in which a system learns from data rather than following predefined rules: inputs and outputs are paired, and the algorithm works out the rules itself. In remote sensing this is valuable because spectral signatures are high-dimensional and often too complex to separate by hand. In supervised learning, the model is trained on a labelled dataset and learns to associate the spectral values of a pixel with a land cover class. This project applies the approach to a portion of the Bolivian rainforest, chosen because Bolivia has had one of the highest rates of tropical deforestation in recent years. The aim was a binary map separating intact forest from deforested or bare soil. To frame the problem, the NDVI composites of 2018 and 2025 were compared. In 2018 the forest is still dominant, though the first linear clearings, in the herringbone pattern typical of agricultural expansion in the Bolivian Amazon, are already visible. By 2025 the landscape is much more fragmented, with wide areas of low NDVI indicating a loss of canopy biomass and an advancing deforestation frontier. The classification was carried out in Google Earth Engine on a median Landsat 8 Level-2 composite of summer 2025, built by filtering scenes by study area and a maximum cloud cover of 50%. Four training polygons were drawn by hand, two for class 0 (non-forest) and two for class 1 (forest), and merged into a FeatureCollection with a “class” property. The sampleRegions() function extracted the pixel values across the visible, near-infrared, shortwave-infrared and thermal bands to build the training dataset. A Support Vector Machine, which separates the two classes with a hyperplane maximizing the margin between them, was trained with a radial basis function kernel and then applied to the whole scene. The resulting map shows extensive non-forest areas arranged in linear and rectangular patterns, the geometric signature of agricultural conversion. They are especially widespread in the central part of the scene and in the upper-left and lower-right corners, while the remaining forest survives in irregular patches, mostly in the upper right. Compared with the satellite layers and the NDVI maps, the classification proves consistent with the fragmented landscape and with the forest conversion processes known for eastern Bolivia.
